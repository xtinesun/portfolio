Email A/B testing as a learning loop for small lists

Kajabi’s most-requested email gap: vary subject, preview, or body; set the win metric and fallback; apply the winner — without overclaiming what a small list can prove.

14 min read
Role

At a glance

  • Gap: 84% expected email A/B from Kajabi — still missing — and most lists are too small for “statistically significant” wins.
  • Bet: A/B as a learning loop — creators pick the win metric and a backup on a tie, instead of pretending certainty.
  • Result: v1–v2 shipped; click-report pivot from interviews; A/B pattern reused on landing pages — no fake certainty on small lists.

My role & constraints

Lead Product Designer on Email A/B with 1 designer, 1 PM, and 2 eng.

Owned: end-to-end UX, competitive review of 8 email tools, testing and interviews, and A/B patterns later reused for landing pages.

Co-led: scope with PM and eng for broadcast and automation email.

Constraint: ship on the existing editor; a fuller redesign stayed a separate track (Decision 04).

Business context

79% of creators use Kajabi email; 59% send at least weekly. They need to see what worked so the next email can do better — yet 30% of high-earning creators said email falls short, and 29% already pay other tools (Mailchimp, ConvertKit, ActiveCampaign) about $187/mo for more advanced email features.

In the Messaging & CRM strategy I co-authored, established creators who love Kajabi ranked email the #2 area to improve. In the third-party tool survey, 84% who saw the email category expected A/B testing from Kajabi — and it was still missing. So we designed email A/B as a simple way for small lists to learn, not a copy of big email platforms’ statistical testing.

Research appendix 3 items

A1 Kajabi creator breakdown

Kajabi groups creators by business maturity. This work targeted the two most established segments: the ones already sending serious email volume and paying elsewhere for depth.

  • Builders: have figured out how to run their knowledge business and are refining the winning formula. May be onboarding first employees and spending more time on admin.
  • Scalers: have built a successful enterprise and want to 10× it. Kajabi’s most financially successful, advanced creators. They need simple tools that create sophisticated experiences for a growing community.

Not in focus Dreamers, Aspiring monetizers, and Hobbyists — earlier-stage segments with less email volume and less third-party email spend.

A2 Features expected from Kajabi

Among creators who saw the email category in the third-party tool survey: A/B sits with the top expected features, but unlike scheduled email and personalization, it was still missing.

A3 Top tools outside Kajabi

29% of creators use marketing/funnel tools outside Kajabi (avg $187/mo). Only 32% named A/B as a “most important” feature on those tools, but 84% expected it from Kajabi.

The problem

Before email A/B testing, creators couldn’t try two versions of an email inside Kajabi before sending to their whole list. Most A/B tools are built for big audiences; most Kajabi creators don’t have that — and many already don’t trust the email numbers they see.

What creators wanted

  • A/B testing built into Kajabi — expected, and still missing.
  • A simple way to learn: try a different subject, preview, or body → see which got more opens or clicks → use the better one → improve the next email. Clear answers (“Did this do better?”).

Why we couldn’t copy other tools

Big email platforms design A/B for marketers with thousands of subscribers. Most Kajabi creators don’t — so we designed for our users (see Insights), not statistical significance theater.

Insights

This came from the Messaging & CRM strategy I co-authored, a survey about tools creators use outside Kajabi, creator interviews, and a review of A/B in 8 email tools.

  1. 01Creators expected A/B testing — and Kajabi still didn’t have it

    84% of creators who saw the email category expected A/B testing from Kajabi. 29% already paid other tools (~$187/mo) for stronger email features; only 32% called A/B their “most important” feature on those tools — so the real gap was catching up inside Kajabi and earning trust, not building a niche power-user feature.

  2. 02Small lists can’t produce statistically confident winners

    Most creators don’t have enough people on their list for a quick, sure winner. Numbers can also disagree (more opens, fewer clicks). Interviews backed a simple job: show what got more engagement — not a stats class.

  3. 03Ambiguous results need creator control, not auto-answers

    Strategy flagged confusing open-vs-click outcomes and low trust in Kajabi analytics. Short tests also favor people who open early — easy to hide behind a confident “winner” label.

  4. 04Creators wanted full tests, not subject-line-only

    The first MVP brief limited v1 to subject + preheader, two versions, and manually sending the winner to everyone else. Strategy and the competitive review pushed further: creators already paid elsewhere to test body/preview, and subject-only wouldn’t close the gap people expected.

  5. 05Waiting on an editor rebuild would have left the gap open

    The email editor was old and fragile, and a redesign looked like a multi-quarter project. Waiting on that rebuild would have left the 84%-expected A/B gap open longer.

Competitive research 8 email service providers

I owned the competitive synthesis — tearing down A/B across Braze, Beehiiv, ConvertKit, HubSpot, Mailchimp, Klaviyo, ActiveCampaign, and Unbounce before locking the PRD. Three implications shaped the product bets:

  • Don’t gate on list size — most “serious” A/B assumes big audiences; educate on what a short window can prove instead (Decision 03).
  • Subject-only isn’t enough — deeper tools put preview/body behind paid tiers (~$187/mo elsewhere); ship subject, preview, or body in v1 at no add-on cost (Decision 02).
  • Honesty over a confident wrong answer — Braze shows when results aren’t decisive and still lets you proceed; creators set metric + fallback (Decision 01).

Full teardown notes

  • Most “serious” A/B assumes big lists — help centers push large audiences and short windows, and open rates look worse than they are if you don’t explain the window. Don’t gate on list size; do educate on what the window can prove.
  • Subject-only is table stakes — lower plans often lock to subject-only; deeper tools put body/preview behind paid tiers creators already buy elsewhere (~$187/mo). Ship subject or preview or body in v1, at no add-on cost.
  • Honesty beats a confident wrong answer — Braze shows non-significance, lets you proceed, and keeps updating after a “winner” — early leaders can lose later. Creators set metric + fallback; system applies after the window (see Decision 01).
  • Explainers and idea prompts — short-window explainers and later coaching prompts, after the core loop is trusted. V1 = trustworthy loop; coaching features follow.

Decisions

01Creators define winning. No fake significance.

Call: Creators set the winning metric (open or click), test duration, and fallback before the test starts. After the window, the higher metric wins and sends to the holdout. If A and B tie, the pre-chosen fallback becomes the winner automatically — same winner UI, no separate “inconclusive” badge.

Why: Same pattern as the Braze teardown: honesty over a confident wrong answer. AI drafts stay a starting point for variants, not a recommended winner. Insights · 02 cover the small-list constraints.

From Insights · 02

02Subject, preview, or body in v1. No add-on tax.

Call: v1 lets creators vary one element per test — subject, preview text, or body — keeping everything else the same, plus duration, as standard features. Side-by-side editing keeps the difference easy to see, so the result teaches something clear.

Why: Creators asked for a full learning loop, not subject-only table stakes. They already pay ~$187/mo elsewhere for that depth. Shipping flexibility at no add-on cost closes the parity gap the survey flagged. Scope change: the first MVP brief limited v1 to subject + preheader; strategy risks and the teardown pulled body into the shipped design direction (see Insights · 01 & 04).

From Insights · 01 · 04

03Safe defaults so a small list still runs a real test

Call: Default split 25% / 25% / 50% holdout, with rounding and guards for tiny recipient sets so creators can’t ship 0/0/100 by accident. Planned in-product copy: short windows only reflect early openers; explain what the window measured — don’t overclaim (see Decision 01). Ties are handled quietly: equal win-metric → the fallback chosen in settings becomes the winner (same winner UI).

Why: Small lists need a real test by default. Ambiguous open-vs-click outcomes and low analytics trust were explicit strategy risks. Explainers belong next to duration and results — not only in a help article.

From Insights · 03

04Ship on the editor we had, not the redesign we wanted

Scoped separately Email editor redesign (newsletter / blog / writing surfaces) · Classic Editor · Automated emails

Call: v1 adds A/B to the existing broadcast email experience on the modern React editor. The fuller editor redesign — and Classic / automated email coverage — wait as their own track.

The tension: Eng pushed back on touching the email editor — outdated and fragile across Rails vs React, a rewrite looked like a multi-quarter sink. I wanted a redesigned editor with more flexible editing and preview so variants could be first-class; that craft bar was right, but pairing a platform rebuild with a net-new learning product would have delayed the 84%-expected gap.

Why this was the right bet: We separated proving the learning model from rebuilding the writing platform. Email A/B shipped on the compose surface we had. The editor redesign was scoped as a separate project so it could be done well — more fluid editing and preview, and reusable beyond email for newsletter, blog, and other text-writing needs across the product. That kept eng risk contained, got creators a trustworthy A/B loop sooner, and still let me define reusable patterns (variants, win metric + fallback, comparative results) that sibling teams used for landing-page A/B.

What we built

A learning loop: variants → options → test → winner

Side-by-side editing keeps subject, preview, and body differences understandable. Creators set the win rule and fallback (Decision 01); the system runs the test and shows what worked — without overclaiming.

01 · Variants

I want to create an A/B test from the start — or convert my broadcast email draft into an A/B test.

Why: creators already writing a broadcast email shouldn’t abandon it to test. I owned this entry path: convert the draft into an A/B (or start A/B fresh), then write version A and a B variation. Side-by-side editing keeps subject, preview, and body differences understandable.

Creators pick the one element to test and write its A and B; the rest of the email stays identical across both versions.

02 · Test options: creators define winning

I want to decide how a winner is chosen — and which version wins if A and B tie.

Why: four controls decide how the test runs. Creators also choose when the first test emails go out.

A/B test distribution — set the size of your test group: 25% / 25% / 50% holdout (default) or 10% / 10% / 90%.

Winning metric — open rate or click rate (click only if the email has links); this determines the version sent to remaining recipients.

Test duration — 1–10 hours after the last test email is sent.

Fallback version — Version A or B applied as the winner if A and B tie on the winning metric. Chosen in settings before the test; no separate inconclusive indicator — results show equal metrics and the fallback marked as winner.

Trust copy (planned next to duration / results): short windows mostly reflect early openers; explain what the window measured — don’t overclaim (Decision 01); a tie quietly uses the fallback you already chose.

03 · Results while the test runs

I want to see how my A/B test performed.

Why: while the test is in progress, creators compare A and B side-by-side (opens, clicks, and email previews). The UI stays comparative — planned helper text that early open rate can shift as the window fills (see Decision 01).

04 · Winner sent to the rest

I want to see how my winning variant performed.

Why: after the window, the winning variant goes to the remaining contacts — including when A and B tied and the pre-chosen fallback was applied as winner (equal metrics; fallback shown as winner, no “inconclusive” label). The report separates the original A/B sample from the winner send so creators can see both what the test measured and how the winner performed at full reach.

Feedback · after v1–v2 shipped

Creators could run a test — but still couldn’t see which links got clicks.

“I need to know which links in the email are being clicked — we’ll put a link on a picture and a link on a button. Are they clicking the picture or the button?”

Creator interview — the ask that drove the pivot

What interviews asked for

  • Click report Show which links got clicks — picture vs button — not a single total.
  • Win by specific CTA Pick a winner from the link that mattered, not only overall click rate.

05 · Click report & win by link

I want to see which links got clicks — and pick a winner from a specific CTA, not only total clicks.

Why: total clicks hide which CTA drove action. The pivot added a clearer click report and let creators win by a specific link (e.g. “Buy Now”) when that was the goal of the test.

What's next

Beyond the shipped broadcast email loop, two bets remained on the Messaging & CRM roadmap:

  1. Automated email A/B Separate project

    A different product track: A/B on automated emails — triggered series where learning compounds across a flow, not a single broadcast email. Same honesty principles; different surface and metrics.

  2. Sender & multi-variable tests Later

    Alternate sender / friendly-from names, and run tests that vary more than one dimension when the broadcast learning loop is trusted.

Outcome

Broadcast email A/B shipped in two releases (v1–v2). After launch, creator interviews changed the roadmap: creators didn’t just want total clicks — they wanted a click report and to pick a winner based on one specific link (like “Buy Now”). We pivoted and shipped that next. The same A/B pattern was later reused for landing pages.

  • Shipped (v1–v2): test subject, preview text, or body; set how the winner is picked and for how long; the winner auto-sends to the rest of the list. After the pivot: click report and win-by-specific-link.
  • Separate track: automated email A/B (for triggered email flows, not one-time broadcasts) is in progress as a different product.
  • Reused elsewhere: landing pages use the same model — compare variants side-by-side, pick a winner, fall back if there’s no clear winner.

Reused on landing pages

I defined the A/B pattern — variants, how a winner is chosen (and a backup on a tie), and clear side-by-side results. Other teams reused it for landing page A/B so they didn’t have to invent the comparison pattern again.

Why it mattered to the business

Email A/B testing was one of the most expected email features — and competitors already had it. Creators were paying about $187/mo for outside tools just to run tests. Building it into Kajabi closed that gap, gave creators a reason to stay, and let them learn what earns opens and clicks without leaving Kajabi.

Metrics we proposed after ship

Not measured yet — baselines from Messaging & CRM research:

  • % of creators using Kajabi email; revenue tied to email
  • Open/click lift on A/B’d vs non-tested emails
  • High-revenue creators unhappy with email (baseline 41%)
  • Spend on outside email tools (baseline ~$187/mo)
  • Creators gaining at least 1 contact / 30 days (baseline 23%)

Impact closure

  • Measured: what we can prove is delivery — broadcast email A/B shipped through v2, and the interview-led pivot (click report and win-by-specific-link) shipped after it. No lift numbers are claimed yet.
  • Not claimed here: GMV (creator sales) or retention lift caused by A/B alone.
  • Future: track how many creators start a test, how many set a win rule before sending, support tickets asking “which email won,” and how often landing-page teams reuse the pattern.

Reflection

What worked:

  • Studied competitors first. ConvertKit showed subject-only tests were the bare minimum; Braze showed honest “no clear winner yet” results. That made the gap clear before we locked scope.
  • Cut scope to ship. When the editor rewrite stalled, we shipped A/B on the existing editor instead of rebuilding it — so creators could start testing sooner.
  • Listened after launch. Interviews asked for a click report and win-by-specific-link. We changed the roadmap and shipped that next.

What I’d change:

  • Explain results from day one, not later. A short test can make open rates look misleading — the UI should have said so in the first prototype.
  • Use creators’ own words earlier. Interviews told us the problem; support tickets told us the exact words creators use for it.
  • Measure from week one — how many creators start a test and set a win rule — instead of proposing metrics after ship.

Tradeoff I still sit with: we delayed a fuller editor redesign (and Classic email) so broadcast A/B could ship through v2 and the click-report pivot. That was the right call for speed and risk, and other teams still reused the patterns on landing pages. Automated email A/B is now a separate project. Side-by-side editing and preview never got the polish I originally wanted.

What I did beyond design:

  • Co-wrote the Messaging & CRM strategy
  • Co-led scope with PM and engineering for broadcast and automated email
  • Owned the 8-tool teardown that pushed v1 beyond subject-only tests
  • Turned post-launch interviews into the click-report pivot
  • Defined the A/B pattern — variants, win rule, fallback, side-by-side results — that landing-page teams reused

Takeaway: An A/B test should teach you something, not just crown a winner (Decision 01). On a small list — say 800 people — a few extra opens could just be luck. So decide before you send: what counts as success (opens or clicks), and what sends if it’s a tie. Then treat the result as a hint for next time, not final proof.
