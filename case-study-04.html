Stopping accidental duplicate orders at checkout

Shoppers on a luxury fashion site were buying the same item in the same size twice without noticing. I traced it to a three-second button state on the product page, then shipped the fix in a deliberate order so the result could be measured, even without analytics.

At a glance

My role & constraints

UX/UI Designer on the Purchase squad, working with a PM and an engineer to redesign the add-to-bag flow from the product page through the cart.

Owned: the problem framing, session-replay research, a 14-retailer competitive audit, design exploration, and the release plan.

Constraint: one data analyst for a company of 200+, and a cart that wasn’t set up for tracking, so there were no numbers to show where duplicates came from.

Success metric: fewer customer experience contacts and returns caused by duplicate-order user error.

The problem

Shoppers were ordering the same item in the same size more than once in a single order, often without noticing until it arrived. Each one meant a support contact, a return, or both. On a luxury site, that also chips away at trust.

The customer experience team had a theory: when the site lagged, the confirmation didn’t appear fast enough, so shoppers tapped again.

“Sometimes you add something to bag and the ‘Item Added to Bag’ pop-up does not come up, so you get click happy and click again.”

— Customer experience team

The original flow

The button changed from “Add to Bag” to “Item Added to Bag” for a few seconds, a mini-cart dropped down, and then everything reset. In the cart, a duplicate showed up only as a “2” in a quantity drop-down.

How might we

…make it obvious, at a glance, that an item is already in the bag?

Research

  1. 01Session replays showed the added state was too short-lived

    In FullStory, duplicates came from slow loads that hid the confirmation, a mini-cart that disappeared after about 3 seconds, and a button that cycled back to “Add to Bag” before people noticed. Returning shoppers also forgot they had added the item in an earlier visit. And in the cart, shoppers reviewing their order didn’t catch the duplicate.

  2. 0214 retailers, 3 patterns worth borrowing

    I audited add-to-bag flows across 14 retailers, went deeper on 7 direct competitors with a similar business model, and narrowed them to the 3 clearest patterns.

  3. 03It isn’t just us

    Baymard Institute’s large-scale checkout testing found the same behavior: shoppers often add duplicates by mistake, and many don’t catch them when they review the cart.

Design exploration

Four directions for the product page, then a rethink of the cart. Each round fixed something the last one left unclear.

01Make the added state persistent

Instead of reverting after a few seconds, the button stays changed for the size that’s in the bag, the way a visited link changes color in search results. Returning shoppers can see it’s already there.

02Give Favorite a real button style

The Favorite control had no outline and didn’t read as a button. A secondary style fixed that without competing with Add to Bag.

03Make a second unit a deliberate choice

After adding, “Add to Bag” becomes “Add One More,” with a direct link to the shopping bag below it. A second unit has to be a deliberate choice, and the path to checkout gets shorter. Scoped to v2 because of engineering capacity.

04One line per unit in the cart

In the cart, each unit gets its own line so a duplicate is impossible to miss. Trade-off: shoppers lose quantity editing in the cart, and long carts get longer. That was worth testing, but not worth shipping blind.

The key decision: ship in phases without data

With no tracking on the cart, shipping both fixes at once would have made it impossible to tell which one worked. So I made the release itself the experiment:

  • v1: product page only. The persistent added state shipped, and the cart stayed as it was, so any change in duplicates could be traced to the product page.
  • Measure with what we had. I set up a feedback loop with the customer experience team, using contacts tagged “duplicate order / user error” as the success metric in place of analytics.
  • v2: decide from v1. The cart redesign and the direct path to checkout were planned based on what v1 showed.

Shopping bag direction for v2: one line per unit. Scroll the image, or click to view it larger.

Outcome

The product page change alone moved the number. That confirmed what the session replays suggested: the problem started on the product page, where the added state disappeared too quickly to notice.

Reflection

  • A small UI detail can carry real business cost. A button state that lasted three seconds was generating support tickets and returns.
  • When data is missing, plan the release so it produces the data. Shipping in a deliberate order made v1 the experiment.
  • Constraints decide the order, not the ambition. The ideal flow stayed on the roadmap, sequenced for when there was capacity to build it.

If I built this today

I’d prototype the persistent added state in code, including the slow-load case, and watch a few shoppers use it before launch. The edge case that caused the problem, a lagging confirmation, is exactly what a static mockup can’t show.
