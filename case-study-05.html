Personalization before the data was ready

Moda Operandi wanted shopping that felt curated for each customer, but the data to power it was still being built. I co-led a 5-day sprint across seven functions to decide what we could personalize now, and what had to wait.

5 min read
Role
Product Designer · Personalization squad
Timeline
5-day sprint · Q1 2019
Team
1 PM · 1 eng · 1 UX designer + 7 functions

At a glance

  • Gap: Shoppers had no way to shape their experience, and full personalization depended on data infrastructure that wasn’t ready.
  • Bet: Bring the experience of shopping with a stylist to customers who don’t have one, starting with features that didn’t depend on mature data.
  • Result: A 3-month roadmap, one fix shipped without testing, and two concepts tested with returning customers. 5/5 testers valued the styling section.

My role & constraints

Product Designer on the Personalization squad. With the PM and a UX designer, I designed and ran a 5-day Google Ventures–style design sprint.

Owned: the sprint plan and facilitation with the PM, sketches and prototypes, and the user test plans.

Constraint: the data infrastructure for personalization was still being built in Q1–Q2, so anything we shipped soon had to work with the data Moda already had.

Business context

ModaOperandi.com lets shoppers buy directly from designers’ runway collections. In early 2019 the company formed a squad to create a shopping experience curated for each customer, with the goal of increasing conversion. My job with the PM was to build a realistic Q2–Q3 design roadmap, find quick wins, and strengthen basic capabilities while the data caught up.

The sprint

We brought in one expert from each part of the business: Client Services, E-commerce, Engineering, Data, and Product Design, with the VP of Product Design facilitating and the PM as decider.

Expert interviews closed knowledge gaps: who our shoppers were, what had already been tried, and what technical constraints blocked “best in class” personalization. We turned those into “How might we” questions, mapped the customer journey, and voted on where to focus.

Focus

Returning customers · mobile · the discovery stage, based on the data team’s signal that mobile use was rising.

  • How might we create a stylist-like experience for customers who don’t have a stylist?
  • How might we understand customers through their browsing, shopping behavior, and favorites?
  • How might we make content easier to discover and search?

Insights: scope to what the data can support

A lightning demo of competitors (ASOS, Spring, Birchbox, TJ Maxx and others) produced a long list of ideas: a style quiz, “because you bought” recommendations, homepages that adapt to weather and location, and a “what’s already in my closet” view. Most of them needed data Moda didn’t have yet.

The call: we put the data-heavy ideas on the roadmap and spent the sprint on features that could work with the data Moda already had.

Decisions

Four concepts went up on the wall: a personalized homepage, like/dislike and “see similar” on the product list page, “How to style it” on the product page, and a better no-results search. The group voted on what to do with each.

01Ship the no-results search fix right away

Low risk, no test needed: shoppers who hit a dead end in search get personalized content instead of an empty page.

02Prototype “see similar” on the product list page

Shoppers find alternatives without leaving the page, the way a stylist would suggest similar pieces.

03Prototype “How to style it” and a thumbs-down on the product page

Shoppers can put outfits together, and a thumbs-down lets them give feedback that shapes future suggestions.

What we tested

We ran moderated usability tests on mobile prototypes with returning customers who had shopped on Moda in the last 6 months. Small qualitative tests, used for direction, not proof.

01 · See similar (product list page, 4 shoppers)

  • 4/4 found the interaction clear and easy to use.
  • 3/4 still started with filter and sort, so “see similar” was a nice-to-have rather than a main entry point.
  • Several expected similar items on the product page, which showed where it should go next.

02 · How to style it + thumbs-down (product page, 5 shoppers)

  • 5/5 liked the styling section because it helped them put outfits together.
  • Only 1/5 noticed the thumbs-down. Most scrolled past the suggestions, which raised a real question: is giving feedback worth the shopper’s effort?
  • Many shoppers focused on product details and never reached the bottom of the page.

Outcome

  • Shipped without testing: the no-results search fix.
  • Prioritized: “How to style it,” planned in phases because styling was still done by hand by stylists.
  • Refined: “see similar,” with a clearer “view all similar” call to action and an exploration of putting it on the product page.
  • Roadmap: a 3-month plan for the squad, with the data-heavy ideas sequenced behind the infrastructure work.

Reflection

What worked: having client services, e-commerce, data, and engineering in one room surfaced post-purchase pain points the product team didn’t know about.

What I’d change: agree on how broad the problem is before day one, and require the decider to be present the whole time.

Why it still matters

“See similar,” styling suggestions, and thumbs-down feedback are the same feedback loops that AI recommendation products depend on. This sprint taught me two questions I still ask when designing for AI: what can we personalize with the data we actually have, and is giving feedback worth the user’s effort?
